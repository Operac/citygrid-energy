How to build a resilient energy mix for a multi-site business

Reliable power is an operating outcome, not a single technology. The right plan starts with what each site must keep running and then combines the most suitable sources around that requirement.

Start with the operational requirement

Map the loads that are critical, the hours they must run, the cost of downtime, and the current quality of grid supply. A hotel, a construction site, and a school may all need dependable power, but their load patterns and risk profiles are different.

Give each source a clear role

Grid power may remain the lowest-cost foundation where it is available. Solar can reduce daytime dependence on purchased energy. Batteries can support short interruptions and improve the use of solar generation. Gas or diesel generation can provide dispatchable backup for longer outages and higher loads.

Plan the handoffs

A resilient system is more than a list of assets. Controls, switching logic, fuel or gas availability, maintenance planning, and operating responsibility must work together. The objective is to prevent a failure in one source from becoming a full-site outage.

Measure what happens next

Track consumption, run hours, cost, downtime, and replenishment cycles. This turns an initial energy design into a plan that can improve as the business grows or operating conditions change.

From reactive fuel buying to predictive replenishment

Emergency purchases often begin with a visibility problem. When teams cannot see consumption clearly, procurement happens late, delivery windows become tighter, and energy planning becomes more expensive.

Create one view across locations

Multi-site organisations need a consistent record of stock levels, deliveries, consumption, generator activity, and unusual changes. A shared view reduces the dependence on scattered calls, messages, and manual spreadsheets.

Establish a normal usage pattern

Consumption should be understood against operating hours, occupancy, production activity, weather, and equipment condition. A sudden change may reflect higher demand, a leak, a measurement problem, or equipment running less efficiently.

Set useful replenishment signals

A good alert accounts for current stock, recent usage, expected demand, delivery lead time, and a practical reserve. The goal is to create enough time for orderly procurement and delivery, not simply to announce that stock is already low.

Turn the data into action

  • Plan recurring supply before it becomes urgent.
  • Compare consumption between similar locations.
  • Identify unusual usage earlier.
  • Improve budgeting with a clearer demand forecast.

What Energy-as-a-Service can mean for a growing business

Reliable energy does not always require a business to purchase, own, and coordinate every asset itself. Energy-as-a-Service can align infrastructure, operations, and payment around the energy outcome a site needs.

Begin with the service level

The first question is not which equipment to buy. It is how much power the operation needs, when it needs it, how much interruption it can tolerate, and how demand may change over the agreement period.

Structure the right combination

A service arrangement may combine grid power, solar generation, battery storage, gas systems, generators, monitoring, and maintenance. The mix should reflect the location, load profile, commercial priorities, and available infrastructure.

Evaluate the commercial model

Customers may pay through an agreed service fee, measured energy consumption, or another project-specific structure. Responsibilities for equipment performance, maintenance, fuel or gas supply, monitoring, and service response should be explicit.

What a useful assessment should cover

  • Current sources, costs, and outage exposure.
  • Critical and flexible loads at each location.
  • Generator capacity, operating hours, and fuel consumption.
  • Available space, grid quality, and options for gas, solar, or storage.
  • Service expectations, contract period, and growth plans.
